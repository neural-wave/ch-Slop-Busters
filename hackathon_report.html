Anonymization for Patient data

@ Neural Wave Hackathon

This report describes an iterative, evaluation-driven development process. Several detection strategies were built, measured against a common evaluation framework and progressively consolidated into a single offline anonymizer.

Team · Cristiano Colangelo · Ketai Qiu · Daniel Travaglia · Alind Xhyra · Davide Molinelli

Problem statement

Recorded doctor–patient dialogues and clinical notes must be stripped of identifying information before any use with cloud-based language models, while preserving the information required to assign the original ICD-10 codes. Inference must run on-premises within 4 GB of VRAM, and the codes themselves must remain unchanged. The central difficulty is ambiguity: Parkinson may denote a disease or a surname, and golfing may be a personal detail or the documented cause of an injury. Privacy protection, preservation of medical information, and originality and scalability are weighted equally.

Methodology: evaluation before integration

Before committing to any technique, the team established a shared evaluation framework that tracks the challenge's metrics: silver-standard labels, fixed data splits, a single scorer and an acceptance gate. Design choices were tuned on one split and progress measured on another, while a held-out test set of unseen formats was scored only once. Every technique discussed in this report, whether regular expressions, named-entity recognition, language models, fine-tuning or learned classifiers, was retained or discarded on the basis of measured results rather than expected benefit.

1Zero-shot baseline with a small language model

The first experiment assessed whether a small model could perform the task end to end. Qwen3.5-2B was run zero-shot through Ollama and vLLM (temperature 0) on segments of about 2,000 characters. It returned personal information as structured JSON spans, which were then replaced deterministically with category tags. Additional components generalized ages and dates (for example "forty-something" or "mid 2020s"), retained terms occurring in the encounter's own ICD-10 descriptions, and logged each response so that policy changes could be re-evaluated without further model calls. With FP8 weights, peak memory remained within 4,000 MiB.

Findings. The model handled dates well (91% removed), but because it processes each segment independently it showed systematic weaknesses:

  • names were not linked across turns and fields, so only 85.7% of patient names were removed;
  • clinical terms were misclassified as organizations or locations ("diabetes" was tagged as an organization; 326 organization tags against 37 actual organizations);
  • 188 of 292 predicted dates were in fact doses or durations, and 8–13 of 400 responses produced malformed JSON.

Baseline: 75.2% of privacy-related information removed; medical concept recall 94.3%.

2Rule-based detection extended with NER

The second phase inverted the approach on the premise that regular identifiers are best handled by deterministic rules. These comprised context-aware regular expressions (dates of birth in more than six formats, record numbers, spoken ages, titles followed by names) and dictionary lookups against public reference lists (ICD-10-CM terms, drug names, census name lists, GeoNames place names).

Named-entity recognition was then layered on top of the rules:

  • GLiNER2, with additional "medication" and "medical concept" labels, so that drugs and diseases no longer compete with organization labels;
  • deid-RoBERTa and GLiNER-PII as alternative recognizers.

From Parallel Prototypes to a Unified Pipeline

3Parallel prototypes and comparative assessment

Four prototypes tackled the same problem: an LLM-first harness (extraction, then generalization), a shield-first rule-based harness (clinical content protected before identifiers are removed), a GLiNER2 pipeline with cross-field entity resolution, and a fully AI-generated implementation. Merged labels and a single scorer (reproducing both originals to four decimals) made them comparable:

Approach (validation, 67 encounters)Privacy-related info removedClinical traps kept

Rules plus an identity graph won on names (100% of name tokens removed); the language model won on occupations and life events. The two were complementary.

Shield-first backbone (shield, rules, identity graph, leak repair) plus the LLM-first strengths (targeted extraction, age generalization, response cache). Two modes: rules (CPU only) and llm (default: rules + qwen3.5:2b via Ollama, preset privacy). A 147 MB container runs fully offline.

Fine-tuning a decision policy (SFT + PPO)

Qwen3.5-2B was trained to decide what to remove, not to rewrite the text. It returns its decisions as JSON and ordinary code applies them, so the ICD-10 codes are never altered. Training used about 1,000 labelled examples; PPO rewarded each correct decision and penalized removing too much. The model passed the gate, but its gains were within measurement noise, so it was not used.

6Learned shield

A "System 1" classifier to protect medical text the rules don't know. More memory, no measured gain: excluded.

7Unseen formats

ICU-note postcodes and dates clashed with numeric shields; fixed, then scored once. Traps kept 95.3%, below the 99.5% floor: the main open gap.

Evaluation Results: ACI-Bench and Kaggle Datasets

Datasets and metrics

ACI-Bench: the evaluation split given by the hackathon challenge, 67 doctor–patient dialogues and notes with ICD-10 codes. Kaggle: open-source medical and synthetic data the team collected (discharge summaries, scribe transcripts in five languages, long dialogues, NBME notes, PMC case reports, essays), without ICD-10 codes and with partial privacy labels.

i.a Privacy-related information removed. Input: original and anonymized text, 538 labelled private items. Share of their occurrences with no distinctive part left. Direct identifiers: names, dates, IDs, contacts.

ii.b Medical concept recall. Input: original and anonymized text, 6,607 labelled medical terms. Share of their occurrences still present.

ii.a F1-Score ICD-10 code prediction. Codes predicted from the original and the anonymized note, scored against the ACI-Bench gold codes: F1 = 2TP / (2TP + FP + FN); Δ = anonymized − original.

ii.a coders. LLM as a judge: Claude Sonnet 5.5, three passes, majority vote per note. Decision tree: trained on the challenge's ACI-Bench training encounters and their codes; predicts 3-character categories.

Same codes. Codes from anonymized text scored against codes from the original; 1 = identical. Used for Kaggle (no gold codes).

iv.a.ii Recovered information. A blinded LLM agent reads the anonymized text and guesses name, age, place and job; share of correct guesses. iv.c: first 10 encounters; VRAM incl. Ollama.

Trade-off between privacy (i.a) and medical concept recall (ii.b)

What the trade-off shows

Each point is one approach: further right means more privacy-related information removed, higher means more medical concepts kept. Ensemble + Generalizer sits at the top right: it keeps almost every medical concept (0.998) while removing 0.814 of the private information.

Replace-All Ensemble removes the most (0.931), especially occupations and organization names, but replacing every detected span also removes medical terms, so its concept recall drops to 0.946.

Conclusions and limitations

  • Protecting clinical content before detection proved more reliable than repairing it afterwards; small LLMs work best where rules are blind, not as the whole pipeline.
  • We chose Ensemble + Generalizer as the final approach: it keeps far more medical concepts than Replace-All Ensemble (ii.b 0.998 vs 0.946) with a slightly higher F1-Score ICD-10 code prediction (ii.a 0.554 vs 0.536, within judge noise), at the cost of removing less privacy-related information (i.a 0.814 vs 0.931).
  • Differences of about 0.10 in the proportion of recovered information (iv.a.ii) are within the variation of a single LLM adversary per set. Record linkage through the ICD-10 codes, which must be kept, makes k-anonymity unattainable.
  • Limitations: all three ACI-Bench label sources were produced with LLM assistance; unseen formats score lower; some date forms are missed.
