Unified anonymization harness

An offline, diagnosis-aware anonymizer for patient–doctor encounters. It removes what identifies a person and keeps what a clinical coder needs, on a laptop, with about 2.8 GB of GPU memory on an NVIDIA card.

Version 1.0.0. Built from saved evaluation results by scripts/build_solution_page.py. All labels are silver (LLM-made, script-checked, not human-reviewed); numbers are shares of labeled occurrences on the named split.

1. The problem and the constraints

Encounter transcripts and notes carry names, dates, places, jobs and small personal details next to the clinical facts that support the ICD-10 codes. The task is to remove the first without damaging the second. Every field except dialogue and note stays byte-identical, including the codes. Inference must run offline, on premises, within 4 GB of VRAM, with a CPU path. A failure must stop the run, never fall back to a weaker mode or skip a record.

2. The central idea

Shield first, then remove. Before any detector runs, a medical shield locks the words a coder needs: clinical numbers, drug names, eponyms such as Parkinson’s, and words that are evidence for the encounter’s own codes. Detectors may then be as eager as they like; nothing they find can cut into the shield.

Use the language model only where it adds privacy. Rules and gazetteers catch the regular cases. A small local model (qwen3.5:2b-q4_K_M via Ollama) reads only the privacy-dense parts of the text (turns and sentences about work, home, family, travel or rare names) and finds what rules miss, such as occupations and employers.

The design merges two earlier team harnesses. From Alind and Davide it takes the shield-first rules backbone, the identity graph and the leak repair. From Ketai it takes LLM extraction, word-level generalization of ages and the replay cache.

3. The pipeline

  1. Normalize. A one-to-one character view (same offsets) masks speaker tags and folds case, so every detector reports positions in the original text.
  2. Shield. Clinical numbers with units, drugs, eponyms in medical syntax, code-evidence words and activities told as an injury mechanism are locked.
  3. Detect. Rules for names after titles, ages, dates, IDs and contact data; gazetteers for places, employers, brands and activities; the local LLM on personal segments (feature F2), with replies cached by model digest, prompt version and segment.
  4. Link. An identity graph joins the mentions of each person across turns and fields, including typos and possessives, so a surname cannot survive in another sentence.
  5. Policy. Each category is removed, generalized or kept according to the preset.
  6. Verify. A leak scan looks for any removed string left in the text and repairs it (two rounds); the output contract is checked record by record.
  7. Publish. Files are written atomically under a dated name, with a decisions sidecar that explains every edit without copying original text.

Original note
CHIEF COMPLAINT

Right knee pain.

HISTORY OF PRESENT ILLNESS

Marta Lindqvist is a 52-year-old female mail carrier from Oakridge who presents with right knee pain after a fall on ice on 01/14/2026. Pain is 6/10 with mild swelling.

VITALS

Blood pressure: 140/90 mmHg.

RESULTS

Hemoglobin A1c: 7.2%.

ASSESSMENT AND PLAN

1. Right knee pain after a fall. Order right knee x-ray.
2. Type 2 diabetes mellitus. Continue metformin 500 mg twice daily.

Dr. Ana Varga
Anonymized note
CHIEF COMPLAINT

Right knee pain.

HISTORY OF PRESENT ILLNESS

[PATIENT] is a [50s]-year-old female [OCCUPATION] from [LOCATION] who presents with right knee pain after a fall on ice on [DATE]. Pain is 6/10 with mild swelling.

VITALS

Blood pressure: 140/90 mmHg.

RESULTS

Hemoglobin A1c: 7.2%.

ASSESSMENT AND PLAN

1. Right knee pain after a fall. Order right knee x-ray.
2. Type 2 diabetes mellitus. Continue metformin 500 mg twice daily.

Dr. [CLINICIAN]

The name, age, occupation, town, date and the clinician’s name are removed or generalized; the blood pressure, HbA1c, metformin dose and “right knee” are shielded and kept.

4. The policy

WhatActionWhy
Names of patients, clinicians, relativesReplaced by a role tag ([PATIENT], [CLINICIAN], …)Direct identifiers
Dates of birth, IDs, phone, e-mail, addressesReplaced by a type tagDirect identifiers
Absolute dates, calendar years, holidaysReplaced; relative timing (“two weeks ago”) keptDate the visit
Exact agesGeneralized to a decade band ([50s])Age-dependent coding still works
Places, facilities, employers, occupations, teamsGeneralized to a category tagQuasi-identifiers
Rare personal details (events, family size, pets, brands)Generalized ([DETAIL]) in the privacy presetLow-risk quasi-identifiers
Hobbies and activitiesGeneralized, unless the sentence tells an injury mechanism (F0)Kept when they are code evidence
Clinical numbers, drugs, eponyms, ICD evidence wordsAlways kept (shield)Utility for ICD-10 coding

Presets trace the privacy–utility curve: conservative, balanced, aggressive, and privacy, the shipped default. privacy uses balanced settings for names and ages, removes low-risk details too, and turns on the code-evidence guard (F0).

5. How it was evaluated

  • Labels. Silver labels from both teams were merged for the 67 evaluation encounters, with 26 conflicts adjudicated. 100 previously unlabeled training encounters were annotated by LLM agents and validated by script. No label was reviewed by a human.
  • Splits. Tune: 140 training encounters, used for every design decision. Validation: the 67 evaluation encounters, checked once per finalist. Test: 24 held-out documents in an unseen format, scored once.
  • Metrics. One scorer computes both teams’ definitions and reproduces both original scorers exactly on nine saved runs. It reports removal by risk tier, direct versus quasi identifiers, name tokens, keep traps, medical concepts and edit precision. A local ICD-10 retrieval proxy measures coding agreement between original and anonymized text.
  • Acceptance gate. A change is kept only if high-risk removal does not drop, quasi or low-risk removal rises, keep traps and concepts stay at or above 99.5%, and ICD agreement stays within noise.

The test set holds 24 ICU progress notes in a format no design decision saw. Scores drop: high-risk removal 89.8%, name tokens 79.8% and keep traps 95.3%, below the 99.5% floor. This is the main open gap: the rules and the identity graph were tuned on dialogue-plus-note encounters. The first test attempt stopped without output because identifiers such as postcodes cut through false number shields; the fix records those shields as overridden instead of failing, and this table is the single scored run after it.

Attacks on the tune output

  • Dictionary attack with every patient name of the corpus plus census names: 5 of 440 labeled names found (1.1%).
  • Rare-word attack: 10 of 47 rare identifier-like tokens survive (78.7% removed).
  • Record linkage by an attacker who already holds the original records: 140 of 140 re-linked. Clinical content is kept by design, so this bounds the threat model: the tool protects against identifying the person, not against matching a record someone already has.

7. Running it

ollama pull qwen3.5:2b-q4_K_M
uv sync
uv run unified-harness doctor
uv run unified-harness anonymize --input data/eval_patient_doctor_encounters.jsonl

Or with Docker: docker compose run --rm harness anonymize --input /data/…jsonl; the harness (147 MB image) and Ollama run on an internal network without internet access. Evaluation: unified-harness evaluate, run, gate and attack.

Limitations

  • All labels are silver; no human review.
  • Some date forms are not targeted (F1 deferred); this is the main high-risk gap on validation.
  • A relative or clinician sometimes receives the [PATIENT] tag; the name itself is removed.
  • GPU memory: 2.82–2.86 GB on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 (nvidia-smi, Ollama runner process), 1.56 GB on Apple unified memory (Ollama's report, weights and cache only). The VRAM column in the tables shows the figure of the machine each run was made on (Apple). A card with exactly 4 GB was not tested.
  • On new text the local LLM costs about 10–14 s per encounter; reruns read the cache.
